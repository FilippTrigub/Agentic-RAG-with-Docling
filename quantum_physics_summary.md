# Quantum Physics: Key Concepts

## 1. Basic Principles of Quantum Mechanics
Quantum mechanics is the fundamental theory that describes the physical properties of nature at the scale of atoms and subatomic particles.

- **Wave-Particle Duality**:
  - Particles (e.g., electrons, photons) exhibit both wave-like and particle-like properties.
  - Described by the **de Broglie hypothesis**: `λ = h/p` (wavelength `λ` is inversely proportional to momentum `p`).

- **Quantization**:
  - Certain properties, such as energy, angular momentum, and spin, are **quantized** (restricted to discrete values).
  - Example: Electrons in an atom occupy discrete energy levels (quantum states).

- **Uncertainty Principle (Heisenberg)**:
  - It is impossible to simultaneously know both the **position** and **momentum** of a particle with absolute precision.
  - Mathematically: `Δx · Δp ≥ ħ/2` (where `ħ` is the reduced Planck constant).

- **Superposition**:
  - A quantum system can exist in multiple states simultaneously until measured.
  - Example: Schrödinger’s cat (a thought experiment illustrating superposition).

- **Entanglement**:
  - Particles can become **entangled**, meaning the state of one particle is instantly correlated with another, regardless of distance.
  - Einstein called this "spooky action at a distance."

- **Wavefunction (ψ)**:
  - A mathematical description of a quantum system’s state.
  - The **Schrödinger equation** governs its evolution over time.
  - The square of the wavefunction (`|ψ|²`) gives the **probability density** of finding a particle in a given state.

## 2. Key Differences from Classical Physics
| **Classical Physics**               | **Quantum Physics**                          |
|-------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------|
| Deterministic (exact predictions)   | Probabilistic (predicts probabilities)      |
| Continuous energy levels            | Discrete (quantized) energy levels          |
| Particles have definite positions   | Particles exist in superpositions           |
| No wave-particle duality            | Wave-particle duality is fundamental        |
| No entanglement                     | Entanglement enables non-local correlations |
| Macroscopic scale                   | Atomic and subatomic scale                  |

## 3. Important Applications and Technologies
Quantum mechanics underpins modern technology and enables breakthroughs in:

- **Electronics & Computing**:
  - **Transistors** (semiconductors, basis of modern electronics).
  - **Quantum Computing**: Leverages qubits (quantum bits) to perform computations exponentially faster for certain problems (e.g., Shor’s algorithm for factorization, Grover’s search algorithm).

- **Cryptography**:
  - **Quantum Key Distribution (QKD)**: Enables theoretically unhackable communication (e.g., BB84 protocol).
  - **Post-Quantum Cryptography**: Developing encryption resistant to quantum attacks.

- **Medical & Imaging Technologies**:
  - **MRI (Magnetic Resonance Imaging)**: Uses nuclear magnetic resonance (quantum property of spin).
  - **Lasers**: Based on stimulated emission, used in surgery, communications, and manufacturing.

- **Materials Science**:
  - **Superconductors**: Materials with zero electrical resistance at low temperatures (used in maglev trains, MRI machines).
  - **Quantum Dots**: Nanoscale semiconductors for displays, solar cells, and medical imaging.

- **Fundamental Physics**:
  - **Quantum Field Theory**: Framework for particle physics (Standard Model).
  - **Quantum Gravity**: Attempts to unify general relativity and quantum mechanics (e.g., string theory, loop quantum gravity).

## 4. Fundamental Experiments Demonstrating Quantum Behavior

### Double-Slit Experiment
- **What it shows**: Wave-particle duality.
- **Setup**: Particles (e.g., electrons, photons) are fired at a barrier with two slits.
- **Result**:
  - If unobserved, particles create an **interference pattern** (wave behavior).
  - If observed (measured), particles behave like **classical particles** (no interference).
- **Implication**: Observation collapses the wavefunction.

### Stern-Gerlach Experiment (1922)
- **What it shows**: Quantization of angular momentum (spin).
- **Setup**: A beam of silver atoms passes through a non-uniform magnetic field.
- **Result**: Atoms deflect in **discrete directions** (up or down), not continuously.
- **Implication**: Spin is quantized.

### Bell Test Experiments (1972–Present)
- **What it shows**: Non-locality and entanglement.
- **Setup**: Entangled particles are measured at distant locations.
- **Result**: Violates **Bell’s inequalities**, proving that quantum mechanics cannot be explained by **local hidden variables**.
- **Implication**: Entanglement is real and non-classical.

### Quantum Tunneling (Observed in Scanning Tunneling Microscope, STM)
- **What it shows**: Particles can traverse energy barriers they classically shouldn’t.
- **Setup**: Electrons tunnel through a vacuum between a sharp tip and a surface.
- **Result**: Enables atomic-scale imaging (e.g., visualizing individual atoms).
- **Implication**: Essential for nuclear fusion, flash memory, and STM.

### Delayed-Choice Experiment (Wheeler, 1978)
- **What it shows**: Retrocausality in quantum mechanics.
- **Setup**: A modified double-slit experiment where the measurement choice is made **after** the particle passes through the slits.
- **Result**: The particle’s past behavior (wave or particle) is determined by a future measurement.
- **Implication**: Challenges classical notions of causality.

## Conclusion
Quantum mechanics revolutionized our understanding of the universe, introducing probabilistic behavior, wave-particle duality, and entanglement. Its principles underpin modern technology, from electronics to cryptography, and continue to inspire cutting-edge research in quantum computing, materials science, and fundamental physics. The field remains one of the most profound and counterintuitive areas of science.
